Chapter 1 · Lesson 13

Bone Anatomy: The Framework Beneath

Before you can classify a fracture, you need to understand the architecture of the bone that broke. The clinical terms you use in orthopedic documentation come directly from bone anatomy — and understanding the structure tells you why different fractures behave differently and require different interventions.

The Five Critical Bone Structures

The periosteumperi- (around) + oste/o (bone)Tough fibrous membrane covering the outer surface of bone. Contains blood supply, nerve fibers, and osteoblasts for repair. is the tough fibrous membrane that wraps the outer surface of every bone. It carries the blood supply and the osteoblasts (bone-forming cells) critical for fracture healing. When a fracture "strips" the periosteum, healing is more complex and often requires surgical intervention. In children, the periosteum is thicker and more flexible — which is why their bones bend before they fully break (greenstick pattern).

Beneath the periosteum is cortical bonecortic/o (outer layer/cortex)Dense outer shell of compact bone. Provides structural strength. Makes up ~80% of total bone mass., also called compact bone — the dense outer shell that provides mechanical strength. When you see "cortical disruption" on a radiology report, that means there is a fracture. The medullary canalmedull/o (middle/marrow)Central hollow cavity running through the diaphysis. Contains yellow marrow in adults. runs through the central shaft, containing bone marrow. In adults it holds yellow marrow (fat); in children and red marrow sites it produces blood cells.

Every long bone has two distinct regions: the epiphysisepi- (upon) + physis (growth)Rounded ends of a long bone. Covered with articular cartilage. Growth plate (epiphyseal plate) is here in children. at each end — covered with articular cartilage for joint function — and the diaphysisdia- (through) + physisThe shaft — the long middle portion of a long bone. Contains the medullary canal., the long shaft. In pediatric patients, fractures involving the epiphyseal plate (growth plate) are a priority concern because they can disrupt normal bone growth.

Lining the inner surface of the medullary canal is cancellous boneLatin: cancelli (lattice)Spongy, porous inner bone at the epiphyses. Houses red marrow. Provides cushioning and flexibility. — also called spongy or trabecular bone. This lattice structure is found at the bone ends and provides a shock-absorbing scaffold. Osteoporosisoste/o (bone) + por/o (pore) + -osisCondition of decreased bone mineral density. DEXA T-score < -2.5. High fracture risk from minimal trauma. preferentially affects cancellous bone, which is why osteoporotic fractures occur at the vertebrae (compression), hip, and wrist — all high-cancellous-bone sites.

Axial vs. Appendicular: Organizing the Skeleton

The 206 bones of the adult skeleton are organized into two divisions. The axial skeleton (80 bones) forms the central axis: skull, vertebral column, ribs, and sternum. The appendicular skeleton (126 bones) includes the limbs and the girdles that connect them to the axial skeleton — the shoulder (pectoral) girdle and the pelvic girdle. Most ER fractures involve the appendicular skeleton; spinal fractures involve the axial skeleton.

Root Word Checkoste/o = bone (osteology, osteoporosis, osteomyelitis). arthr/o = joint (arthritis, arthroscopy, intra-articular). chondr/o = cartilage (chondral, chondromalacia). cortic/o = outer layer (cortical bone). medull/o = marrow/middle (medullary canal). peri- = around (periosteum).

Fracture Classification: Six Patterns You Must Know

Fracture classification is not optional vocabulary — it directly determines treatment decisions. A greenstick fracture in a child is managed very differently from a comminuted fracture in an adult, and an open fracture requires immediate surgical intervention that a closed fracture does not. Your ability to identify these patterns from an X-ray description is a Board Exam core skill.

Fracture Descriptor Terms

Beyond fracture type, you must document additional descriptors. A fracture is displaceddis- (apart) + placedFracture fragments are no longer aligned — bone segments are offset from each other. when the fragments are no longer aligned (vs. non-displaced, where the fracture line is present but the bone maintains position). A fracture is open (compound) when bone breaks through skin — this immediately elevates infection risk because the skin barrier is breached, creating a direct pathway for bacteria to reach bone, causing osteomyelitisoste/o + myel/o (marrow) + -itisBacterial infection of bone and bone marrow. Serious complication of open fractures requiring prolonged IV antibiotics.. A fracture is intra-articular when the fracture line extends into the joint space — this carries higher complication risk because articular cartilage is involved.

ORIF IndicationORIFOpen Reduction Internal FixationSurgery to realign bone (reduction) and hold fragments with hardware — plates, screws, intramedullary nails. (Open Reduction Internal Fixation) is indicated when: displacement exceeds 2mm, fracture is intra-articular, fracture is open, or conservative management has failed. Document: "ORIF of comminuted intra-articular distal radius fracture — consent obtained."

Crepitus: Assess Once, Document, Move On

CrepitusLatin: crepitare (to crackle)Grating or crackling sensation from fractured bone ends rubbing. Assess ONCE during initial eval — never reproduce intentionally. is the grating sensation of bone ends moving against each other. It confirms fracture but causes significant pain and can worsen displacement. Clinical rule: assess it once during initial examination, document it, then immobilize immediately. Never intentionally reproduce crepitus in subsequent assessments.

Chapter 3 · Lesson 14 — Splinting Lab

CMS Assessment: The Neurovascular Check That Protects Your Patient

Every extremity fracture that receives a splint or cast requires a CMS assessmentCirculation, Motion, SensationNeurovascular check before and after splinting to detect distal complications — circulatory compromise or nerve damage. both before and after the intervention. This is not optional documentation — it is a standard of care and a legal protection. A deteriorating CMS finding after splinting means the splint must be loosened or bivalved immediately.

C — Circulation

Assess capillary refill (should be less than 2 seconds — press the fingernail, release, count until pink returns), skin color (should match the uninjured side), and skin temperature (should be warm). Pale, cool skin with refill greater than 2 seconds = compromised circulation = emergency intervention required.

M — Motion

Ask the patient to wiggle their fingers or toes on the affected side. Can they perform active distal digit motion? Inability to move distally — with normal sensation — may indicate tendon or nerve injury distal to the fracture or splint. Inability plus abnormal sensation suggests vascular or nerve compromise.

S — Sensation

Apply light touch to the dorsal and palmar/plantar surfaces. Compare bilateral. Numbness or tingling (paresthesia) distal to a splint suggests nerve compression — loosen the splint immediately. Document as: "Sensation: light touch intact dorsal and palmar surface right hand bilaterally. No paresthesia reported."

Documentation StandardPre-splint CMS: "Circulation: cap refill 1.5 sec, digits warm and pink. Motion: all digits flex/extend actively. Sensation: light touch intact, no paresthesia." Post-splint CMS: repeat all three components. Any change = immediate intervention.

FOOSH: The Most Common Fracture Mechanism in the ER

FOOSHFall On Outstretched HandClassic mechanism for distal radius, scaphoid, and elbow fractures. Hand extended to catch fall. — Fall On Outstretched Hand — is the single most common fracture mechanism in the ER, especially in children and older adults. When a patient falls and instinctively extends their hand to catch themselves, the impact force travels up through the wrist and forearm. Classic FOOSH injuries: distal radius fracture (most common), scaphoid fracture (watch for tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox), and radial head fracture.

Task 1 · Pre-Splint CMS Assessment
The attending orders a posterior splint before the X-ray results are back. Write the PRE-SPLINT CMS findings as they should appear in the Objective section of the SOAP note. Use clinical documentation format. Assume: cap refill 2s, toes wiggle but with discomfort, light touch intact bilaterally.
Format: C = describe cap refill time + skin color/temp. M = state whether patient can actively move distal digits. S = describe light touch findings bilaterally. Each component should be one complete clinical sentence. Compare to the uninjured side where applicable.
Task 2 · Fracture Classification from X-Ray Description
The radiology report comes back: "Oblique fracture line at the distal fibula with 2mm lateral displacement of the distal fragment. Cortex disrupted through full thickness. No involvement of the ankle joint space. Soft tissue swelling noted." Classify the fracture using the correct descriptor terms from your formulary. Explain each classification.
Oblique = diagonal fracture line (similar to spiral but without the helical wrap). Full thickness cortex = complete fracture. 2mm displacement = displaced (fragments have moved). Skin intact = closed. "No involvement of ankle joint space" = extra-articular. Use these four descriptors: pattern + displacement status + open/closed + articular involvement.
Task 3 · ORIF Decision
The attending considers conservative management (casting) vs. ORIF. The fracture is displaced 2mm. Based on the ORIF indication criteria from your reading, would ORIF be indicated in this case? Justify your answer using clinical evidence from the scenario.
ORIF thresholds: displacement >2mm, intra-articular extension, open fracture, or failed conservative management. This fracture has exactly 2mm displacement — this is the threshold. The fracture is extra-articular and closed. The decision in borderline cases often depends on: patient age, activity level, and whether the fracture can be maintained in alignment with casting alone.
Task 4 · Post-Splint Documentation + SOAP Assessment
After splinting, the post-splint CMS shows: cap refill 3s, toes pale and cool, patient reports tingling in the dorsum of the foot. Write: (1) the post-splint CMS documentation and (2) the immediate clinical response you would initiate and why.
Cap refill 3s = ABNORMAL (>2s = compromised circulation). Pale/cool = abnormal color/temp = circulation impaired. Tingling in dorsum = paresthesia = sensation abnormal. 2 of 3 CMS components abnormal. Response: loosen/bivalve the splint IMMEDIATELY. This is a time-sensitive emergency — compartment syndrome is a risk.
