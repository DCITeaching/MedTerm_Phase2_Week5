The Skeletal System:
Reading Bones, Classifying Fractures

The skeleton is the framework every other system depends on. Before you can classify a fracture, manage an orthopedic injury, or read a radiology report, you need to know the names, locations, and clinical significance of the major bones — and the precise vocabulary used to describe them.

What
Name and locate all major bones, classify 6 fracture types, perform CMS checks, document ORIF indication
Why
Interns must read radiology reports, communicate with orthopedic consultants, and document fractures precisely — errors are patient safety risks
How Assessed

Patient: Marcus Williams, 17M — Basketball Injury

Marcus arrives after landing awkwardly during basketball. He heard a pop and cannot bear weight on his right ankle. Assessment: significant swelling and ecchymosis over the lateral malleolus (distal fibula). Bony tenderness on palpation at the distal fibula. X-ray ordered. Mechanism: inversion injury on landing.

Task 1 · Bone Identification
The X-ray report reads: "Oblique fracture at the distal fibula with 2mm lateral displacement." Using your bone knowledge from this week: (a) Where is the fibula in relation to the tibia? (b) What is the "distal" portion of the fibula called clinically, and what bony landmark is commonly palpated here? (c) Which skeletal division does the fibula belong to — axial or appendicular?
The fibula is the LATERAL (outer) bone of the lower leg — the tibia is the larger, medial (inner) bone and the primary weight-bearing bone. The distal fibula forms the lateral malleolus — the bony bump on the outside of your ankle. The fibula belongs to the appendicular skeleton (limbs and girdles).
Task 2 · Pre-Splint CMS Documentation
Before applying a posterior splint, you perform a CMS check. Findings: capillary refill 1.5s, toes move with mild discomfort, light touch sensation intact bilaterally. Write the pre-splint CMS in SOAP Objective format. Then describe what would change in your response if post-splint cap refill were 4s and the patient reported numbness.
Pre-splint normal: "Circulation: cap refill 1.5s, toes warm and pink bilaterally. Motion: all toes flex/extend on command with mild discomfort. Sensation: light touch intact dorsal and plantar foot bilaterally, no paresthesia." Post-splint abnormal response: loosen/bivalve the splint IMMEDIATELY. Cap refill >2s = impaired circulation. Numbness = impaired sensation. Two of three CMS components abnormal = emergency action required.
Task 3 · Fracture Classification
The radiology report reads: "Oblique fracture line at distal fibula, 2mm lateral displacement of distal fragment, cortex disrupted through full thickness, no skin breach, no involvement of ankle joint space." Using the four fracture descriptor terms from your reading, classify this fracture completely.
Pattern: oblique (diagonal line — similar to spiral but without helical wrap). Displacement: displaced (2mm offset documented — fragments are not aligned). Open/closed: closed (no skin breach mentioned — skin integrity intact). Articular: extra-articular (no involvement of ankle joint space stated explicitly in report).
Task 4 · ORIF or Conservative Management?
The attending is deciding between casting and ORIF for Marcus's 2mm displaced fibula fracture. Using the ORIF indications from your reading, argue both sides: when would you recommend ORIF, and when would conservative management (casting) be appropriate? What additional information about Marcus would change your recommendation?
ORIF thresholds: displacement >2mm, intra-articular fracture, open fracture, failed conservative management. This fracture is AT the threshold (exactly 2mm, extra-articular, closed). Conservative management factors: patient compliance with non-weight-bearing, ability to maintain alignment in a cast, low-demand activity level. Additional information: is Marcus a competitive athlete (high demand = lean ORIF)? Can the fracture be maintained in alignment with cast? Is there ligamentous injury (high ankle sprain = less stable, lean ORIF)?
